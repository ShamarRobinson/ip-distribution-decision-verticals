Introduction

Ten ideas you will see in this deck, explained simply

Intellectual property (IP)

What it is: Something you created that the law protects, like a book, song, course or design.

Think of it as: Like owning the rights to a song you wrote: others need your permission to copy or sell it.

How it is used: Registering a copyright with the U.S. Copyright Office is what lets you enforce it in court.

Distribution channel

What it is: A company that sells your work to buyers, such as Amazon KDP or IngramSpark.

Think of it as: The stores and services between you and your buyer, the way an app store sits between developers and users.

How it is used: Every industry picks channels: apps use app stores, bands use streaming.

Gross-to-net and unit economics

What it is: The path from the price a buyer pays to the money you keep, one sale at a time.

Think of it as: A receipt showing every cut taken out of the price before you get paid.

How it is used: Pharma, consumer goods and music all track gross-to-net.

Contribution margin

What it is: Money left from one sale after the costs of making that sale.

Think of it as: What each sale adds toward paying off your launch costs.

How it is used: Restaurants and retailers price menus and products with it.

Cost variance analysis

What it is: Explaining why one result costs more than another by splitting the gap into causes.

Think of it as: Pinpointing exactly which cost made your take-home smaller than another option's.

How it is used: Finance teams use it every month to compare plan vs actual.

Breakeven analysis

What it is: How many sales it takes to earn back what you spent to start.

Think of it as: The sale that finally pays back everything you spent to launch.

How it is used: Startups, factories and new product launches all use it.

Sensitivity and scenarios

What it is: Changing one number, or a few at once, to see what moves the result.

Think of it as: Asking 'what if?' before you commit money.

How it is used: Budget planning, investment cases and infrastructure finance.

A/B testing

What it is: Comparing two versions fairly to see which one really works better.

Think of it as: Testing two video thumbnails and keeping the one more people click.

How it is used: Websites, apps and marketing teams run these daily.

Cash timing (DSO)

What it is: How many days pass between a sale and the money reaching your bank.

Think of it as: A job that pays you a month after you work the shift.

How it is used: Every business plans cash this way to pay its bills.

Pool-based payouts

What it is: Getting paid a share of one shared pot, split by usage.

Think of it as: Splitting a team's prize money by how many points each player scored.

How it is used: Music streaming royalties and ad networks pay this way.

Executive summary

Three findings drive the decision

$6.79 vs $3.33

The store's cut drives the gap

On a $16.99 paperback, Amazon KDP leaves the creator $6.79 and IngramSpark's bookstore route leaves $3.33. About $2.55 of that $3.47 gap is the store's discount. See the variance.

158 vs 272

Payback depends on contribution per sale

KDP earns back Year-1 costs after 158 sales of one print plus one ebook; IngramSpark needs 272. Platform fees alone are earned back within 42. See breakeven.

16.6 / month

Flat fees only win at volume

A $13.99 monthly plan beats per-sale fees only above about 17 sales of each format a month. See the crossover.

Decision ask: launch on two channels (KDP plus one route picked from your decision path), keep print non-returnable at launch, and run one 90-day A/B test per quarter. Go to the recommendation.

Decision walkthrough

How to read the decision tree

  1. Start at the top diamond. Ask where most of your buyers will come from: stores and online retailers, or channels you own like an email list, events or a website.
  2. Store path: if Amazon alone is enough, you land on Path A. If you need bookstores or libraries, ask how many you sell each month.
  3. Volume check: under about 17 sales of each format a month, a per-sale wholesaler is cheaper (Path B). Above it, a flat monthly plan wins (Path C).
  4. Own-channel path: if you already have an audience, sell direct and keep the store's slice (Path D). If you are ebook-first, spread across every ebook store (Path E).
  5. Then: answer the two follow-on questions on exclusivity and returns.

The 17-a-month switch point comes from the crossover analysis.

Decision walkthrough · Path A

If you are an independent creator: here is your path

Option 1 (recommended)KDP print + ebook
Option 2 (test arm)KDP + Draft2Digital for other ebook stores
Option 3 (stretch)KDP + KDP Select (Kindle Unlimited)

Who you are: One work, discovered mostly on Amazon, with a small budget.

$6.79kept per $16.99 paperback on KDP
158sales (print + ebook) to break even
$5,961Year-1 money kept at 50 sales a month

Summary and insight

Amazon is where your readers already shop, and KDP keeps the biggest slice of each sale, so it pays back your start-up costs fastest. Adding bookstore distribution now would cut what you keep per book in half without a plan to get shelf space.

What to do

  • Do now: Publish print and ebook on KDP; price the ebook between $2.99 and $9.99 to earn 70%.
  • Do now: Register the copyright before launch and budget for the $85 fee.
  • Do now: Skip KDP Expanded Distribution; it keeps only $3.40 per copy.
  • Test next: Run a 90-day price test ($16.99 vs $14.99); keep the lower price only if sales rise more than 21%.
  • Watch: Watch breakeven progress monthly; when you pass 158 sales, consider adding a wide ebook route (Path E).

Decision walkthrough · Path B

If you are a small press or imprint: here is your path

Option 1 (recommended)KDP + IngramSpark, 55% returnable
Option 2 (test arm)KDP + IngramSpark, 40% non-returnable
Option 3 (stretch)KDP + B&N Press + IngramSpark

Who you are: A curated list that needs bookstore and library placement.

$3.33kept per copy at a 55% discount
272sales to break even
105 daysaverage wait for IngramSpark cash

Summary and insight

Bookstores expect a 55% discount and the right to return books, which shrinks what you keep and adds risk. In exchange you reach 45,000+ outlets that KDP cannot. KDP still handles Amazon, where it pays twice as much per copy.

What to do

  • Do now: Use KDP for Amazon and IngramSpark for bookstores and libraries.
  • Do now: Start IngramSpark titles non-returnable; switching later leaves a 180-day return tail.
  • Do now: Keep a cash reserve for the 90-day payment lag.
  • Test next: Test 55% returnable against 40% non-returnable; returnable must sell 77% more copies to come out ahead.
  • Watch: Reconcile IngramSpark statements monthly for returns and offsets; revisit Path C if any title passes 17 sales of each format a month.

Decision walkthrough

Two follow-on decisions for every path: exclusivity and returns

Exclusivity, in plain words: Kindle Unlimited is like a library pass that pays authors from a shared pot. You must sell the ebook only on Amazon for 90 days. Choose it if your genre lives there and you can handle pay that changes monthly; otherwise stay wide. 38% of surveyed authors keep every book exclusive and 30% stay fully wide.

Returns, in plain words: letting stores return unsold books gets you on more shelves, but you pay for the books that come back, and that bill can be bigger than what you earned. Start non-returnable unless shelf space is the main goal and you have cash set aside.

Recommendation

Decision request: two channels now, one test every 90 days

1. Launch

Every path lists on KDP plus the one route your decision path picks. Keep print non-returnable at launch and turn on distribution only on release day.

2. Test

One A/B test per 90 days: price first, then store terms. Write down the metric (money kept per visitor) and the breakeven lift before starting, and do not stop early.

3. Review

Hold a monthly variance review and a quarterly re-forecast. Revisit exclusivity and subscriptions once sales pass 17 of each format a month.

Success measures: breakeven reached within the planned number of sales; money kept per sale within 5% of plan; cash received within the modeled wait; one decision-grade A/B result per quarter.

Methodology

How the numbers were built

Model

  • One Python model (analysis/model.py) writes every dataset in /data; the deck, Excel model and Tableau story read the same numbers.
  • Base case: $16.99 paperback, 200 pages, $4.99 ebook (2 MB), equal sales per platform.
  • Variance = platform's money kept minus KDP's, split into store share, production and fee drivers that add up exactly.
  • A/B sample sizes use a two-proportion z-test (95% confidence, 80% power).

Assumptions to replace

  • Print costs for IngramSpark ($4.00), B&N Press ($3.50) and Lulu ($5.00) are planning estimates.
  • The $1,500 launch budget, the 65/35 channel mix, the launch-decay curve and Path D's own-store breakeven are illustrative.
  • All prices, fees and rates are as of October 11, 2026. The data is refreshed every three months; next refresh January 11, 2027.
  • Status note: certain works referenced are in the initial stage of pre-publication copyright registration with the U.S. Copyright Office.

How the numbers work

KPI (key performance indicator) definitions, part 1: money kept per sale

KPIWhat it tells youFormulaWorked example
KPI 1. Net royalty per copy
what the creator keeps from one sale		The money left for the IP (intellectual property) owner after the store, the printer and the platform are paid. The main measure in this story.Net royalty = list price - store's cut - printing cost - platform feeKDP paperback: $16.99 - $6.80 - $3.40 - $0.00 = $6.79. IngramSpark: $16.99 - $9.34 - $4.00 - $0.32 = $3.33.
KPI 2. Net margin %
share of the list price the creator keeps		Lets you compare formats and prices on one scale.Net margin % = net royalty / list priceKDP paperback: $6.79 / $16.99 = 40.0%. KDP ebook: $3.28 / $4.99 = 65.8%.
KPI 3. Channel share (store's cut)
also called the take rate		The part of the price the store or wholesaler keeps. It is the biggest cost on almost every route.Store's cut = list price x (1 - royalty rate), or list price x wholesale discountKDP pays a 60% print royalty, so the store keeps $16.99 x 40% = $6.80. IngramSpark's 55% wholesale discount: $16.99 x 55% = $9.34.
KPI 4. Cost variance vs KDP
gap to the Amazon KDP (Kindle Direct Publishing) baseline, split by cause		Shows how much more or less each route keeps than KDP, and why.Variance = route's net - KDP net = store-cut driver + printing driver + fee driver (each driver = KDP's cost - the route's cost)IngramSpark: store cut -$2.55 + printing -$0.60 + fee -$0.32 = -$3.47 per copy.
KPI 5. Contribution per unit pair
contribution margin for one print sale plus one ebook sale		What one reader buying both formats adds toward paying back launch costs.Contribution per pair = print net royalty + ebook net royaltyKDP: $6.79 + $3.28 = $10.08. IngramSpark: $3.33 + $2.97 = $6.30.
KPI 6. Breakeven point (unit pairs)
sales needed to earn back year-1 fixed costs		How many print-plus-ebook sales it takes before the work is profitable. Lower is better.Breakeven = Year-1 fixed costs / contribution per pair, rounded up. Fixed costs = platform setup + $85 copyright registration + $1,500 launch budget + 12 x monthly feeKDP: $1,585 / $10.08 = 157.3, rounded up to 158. IngramSpark: $1,710 / $6.30 = 271.6, rounded up to 272.
KPI 7. Margin of safety
cushion between planned sales and breakeven		How far sales could fall before you stop covering costs.Margin of safety = (planned sales - breakeven) / planned salesKDP at 600 planned pairs: (600 - 158) / 600 = 73.7%.
Worked with the October 11, 2026 numbers. Worked examples use unrounded values from the model, so a shown sum can differ from its parts by a cent. Reference

Glossary of analytics and FP&A terms

A/B test
A fair comparison of two versions on one measure, run long enough to trust the result.
Breakeven lift
How many more sales a cheaper option needs just to match the original.
Breakeven point
Sales at which total money kept equals start-up costs.
Channel share / take rate
The part of the price the store or marketplace keeps.
Coefficient of variation (CV)
How much something swings, compared to its average.
Contribution margin
Money kept from one sale after that sale's own costs.
Cost variance analysis
Explaining a cost gap by splitting it into its causes.
Days sales outstanding (DSO)
Average days between a sale and the cash arriving.
Gross-to-net (GTN)
The steps from list price to the money you keep.
Margin of safety
How far sales can drop before you stop covering costs.
Pool-based payout
Pay that comes from a shared pot split by usage.
Returns reserve
Money held back to cover books that may be returned.
Scenario analysis
Comparing results under slow, normal and great outcomes.
Sensitivity analysis
Changing one input at a time to see which matters most.
Working capital gap
Money earned but not yet collected.
